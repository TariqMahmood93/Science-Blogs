Almost every real dataset has holes in it. A survey question is skipped, a sensor drops out, a hospital records fewer tests for some patients than others. Before most machine learning models can use such data, the holes have to be handled: either the incomplete rows are dropped or the missing values are filled in, a step called **imputation**.

## Not all gaps are the same

<Evidence kind="established">
Statisticians distinguish three situations. Data can be missing completely at random, unrelated to anything; missing at random, where the chance of a gap depends on other recorded values; or missing not at random, where it depends on the missing value itself<Cite id="rubin-mechanisms" />.
</Evidence>

The distinction matters because most simple fixes are only safe in the first case, and the first case is the rarest in practice.

## How a gap becomes a bias

Imagine income is missing more often for one group of applicants. If those rows are dropped, the model sees fewer examples from that group. If the gaps are filled with the overall average, everyone in that group is pulled towards a value that describes someone else.

Either way, the model's errors may now fall unevenly across groups, even though no one intended it.

## What the evidence says

<Evidence kind="interpretation">
Studies that compare imputation methods on fairness metrics suggest the choice of method can change disparities between groups, sometimes as much as the choice of model. Summarise only after checking the sources<Cite id="fairness-imputation" />.
</Evidence>

## What practitioners can do

- Report how much data is missing, broken down by group.
- Compare more than one imputation method and report fairness metrics for each.
- Treat "why is this missing?" as a question about the world, not only about the data.
