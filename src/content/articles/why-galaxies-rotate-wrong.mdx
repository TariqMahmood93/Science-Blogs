This article explains an **established measurement** and lays out two **competing interpretations** of it. The measurement is settled; the explanation is not.

## What is actually measured

If you plot how fast stars orbit the centre of a spiral galaxy against their distance from that centre, Newtonian gravity predicts the curve should fall off at the edge, the way planets slow down as they move outwards in the Solar System. Across hundreds of galaxies, it doesn't<Cite id="rotation-curves" />. Stars at the edge move about as fast as stars midway in.

<Evidence kind="established">
Rotation curves of spiral galaxies stay flat far beyond the visible disk. This has been measured independently in optical light, in 21-centimetre hydrogen emission and in gravitational lensing by galaxy clusters.
</Evidence>

## Two ways out

The data forces one of two conclusions: either there is a lot of mass we cannot see, or the law of gravity is wrong at galactic scales.

1. **Dark matter.** A cold, non-luminous component outweighs the visible stars and gas, and its gravity holds the fast-moving outer stars in. This idea slots cleanly into the standard cosmological model, which also explains the pattern of the cosmic microwave background and the distribution of galaxies<Cite id="lambda-cdm-review" />.
2. **Modified gravity.** The law relating force to acceleration changes at the tiny accelerations found at the outskirts of galaxies. In its original form (MOND) and its relativistic successors, this reproduces rotation curves without new matter, but struggles with cluster-scale observations<Cite id="mond-tests" />.

<Evidence kind="interpretation">
The weight of evidence currently favours dark matter, mainly because it also accounts for observations at scales much larger than individual galaxies. Modified gravity remains a serious research programme because no dark-matter particle has been directly detected despite decades of searching.
</Evidence>

## What would change our mind

- A direct, reproducible detection of a dark-matter particle in a terrestrial experiment.
- A modified-gravity theory that matches every independent line of cosmological evidence, not just rotation curves.
- A galaxy that defies both frameworks, requiring a third explanation.

<Evidence kind="open">
If a direct dark-matter particle detection never arrives, how long should the community keep looking before taking modified-gravity accounts more seriously?
</Evidence>
