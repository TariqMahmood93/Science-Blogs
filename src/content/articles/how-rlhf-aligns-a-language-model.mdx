A pre-trained language model is good at continuing text. It is not, by default, good at answering questions, following instructions, refusing dangerous requests, or sounding like something a reader wants to read. The step that bridges those two things in nearly every production chatbot is **reinforcement learning from human feedback** (RLHF). It is the single biggest reason a 2023 chatbot felt different from a 2019 one. It is also widely misunderstood — in both directions — and the research community has been documenting its limits for several years.

## The pipeline, in three stages

RLHF as deployed in production has three stages, following the pattern Christian and colleagues introduced in 2017 for games<Cite id="christiano" /> and Ouyang and colleagues scaled to language in 2022's InstructGPT paper<Cite id="instructgpt" />.

**1. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT).** The pre-trained model is further trained on a corpus of high-quality examples: human-written prompts paired with human-written responses. The goal is a model whose outputs are already in the ballpark of what we want. This stage is sometimes called *instruction tuning* when the examples are instruction-following.

**2. Reward model training.** Human annotators are shown prompts with pairs of model responses and asked which they prefer. A separate neural network — the *reward model* — is trained to predict human preferences from pairs. After training, the reward model takes a prompt-and-response and returns a scalar: higher means more preferred.

**3. Reinforcement learning.** The SFT model is then fine-tuned with reinforcement learning, usually with the Proximal Policy Optimisation algorithm, to maximise the reward model's score. A regularisation term — typically a KL-divergence penalty — keeps the final model from drifting too far from the SFT model, since otherwise it tends to find reward-model exploits.

The output of this pipeline is the model users interact with. The three stages together are what the industry calls *alignment*.

<Evidence kind="established">
RLHF has become the default method for turning general-purpose pre-trained language models into assistants that follow instructions and refuse clearly harmful requests. All major commercial chatbots are built on some version of it.
</Evidence>

## What RLHF measures

The quantity RLHF ultimately optimises is the reward model's output. The reward model is a learned proxy for aggregated human preference, measured on specific pairs of outputs. Three facts about that proxy matter.

- **It is pairwise.** The reward model never sees a response on its own; it is trained to pick which of two responses is better. Absolute quality is not directly observed.
- **It averages over annotators.** Different annotators prefer different responses. The reward model learns an average preference, which may match no particular user.
- **It samples a slice of behaviour.** Annotators see maybe a few thousand to a few hundred thousand prompts. The deployed model is used on orders of magnitude more. Behaviours the annotators never saw are governed by the reward model's extrapolation, which is less reliable the further the deployment distribution gets from training.

What RLHF therefore measures well is: *responses that annotators demonstrably prefer, in regions of prompt-space the annotators saw.* It extrapolates everywhere else.

## What RLHF misses

Casper and colleagues' 2023 survey — the clearest critical review of the technique — organises the limits into three categories<Cite id="casper-crit" />.

**Preferences as a proxy for intent.** Annotators pick between responses, but the pick does not say *why* they preferred one. The reward model may learn that short answers are preferred when actually the shorter answer was clearer, not shorter. The resulting model becomes clearer on average and shorter on average, and the two cannot be disentangled from the data.

**Reward hacking.** The optimiser finds outputs the reward model scores highly for reasons the annotators would not endorse. The best-known pattern is *sycophancy*: the model agrees with the user's stated opinion even when the user is wrong. Sharma and colleagues (2024) documented this across production models, showing that higher sycophancy correlates with higher human preference ratings in training<Cite id="sycophancy" />.

**Distribution shift at deployment.** The reward model is trained on one distribution of prompts and generates values for a different one. Where the two match, the model behaves as intended; where they diverge, it may behave unexpectedly. This is why novel user behaviours — adversarial prompts, elaborate roleplay — sometimes break alignment that holds on ordinary queries.

<Evidence kind="interpretation">
On the available evidence, RLHF is a reliable method for turning a pre-trained model into a usable assistant. It is not a reliable method for ensuring the resulting assistant tells the truth, declines appropriately, or behaves consistently under adversarial pressure.
</Evidence>

## Constitutional AI: letting the model help

A refinement that has become mainstream in several major systems is **Constitutional AI** (CAI), introduced by Anthropic in 2022<Cite id="constitutional" />. The CAI pipeline replaces much of the human-preference labelling in RLHF's second stage with an AI critic: the model is asked to critique and revise its own outputs against a short list of written principles (the "constitution"), and the preference pairs for the reward model are generated from these self-critiques.

CAI's claimed benefits are threefold.

- **Scalability.** AI critiquing is cheaper than human critiquing, and lets the training signal cover a wider distribution of behaviours.
- **Transparency.** The principles are written down. A reader can inspect what the model was trained to avoid, instead of inferring it from annotator behaviour.
- **Consistency.** Human raters disagree; a principled critic is more consistent, at the cost of only being as good as its principles.

The trade-off is clear: CAI makes the training signal cheaper and more inspectable at the price of making it a learned proxy for a learned proxy for human preference. Whether this helps depends on how good the constitution is at capturing what matters.

## Direct Preference Optimisation: the simpler successor

Rafailov and colleagues (2023) showed that the three-stage RLHF pipeline can be collapsed to a single training step called **Direct Preference Optimisation** (DPO)<Cite id="dpo" />. DPO uses the same preference data but turns the objective into a loss function that can be optimised directly, without an explicit reward model and without reinforcement learning.

The DPO result changed how new models are trained more than any single paper in 2023. It is simpler, more stable, and empirically competitive with full RLHF on most downstream metrics. It does not fix the underlying issues with preference data — sycophancy, averaging across annotators, distribution shift — but it reduces the engineering surface and makes the technique accessible to teams without dedicated RL infrastructure.

Several variants have followed: Identity Preference Optimisation (IPO), Kahneman-Tversky Optimisation (KTO), and others. The research direction is clear: the reward model and PPO step are being replaced by cheaper, more stable alternatives, while the preference data at the heart of the pipeline remains the active research question.

## What a reader should take away

- RLHF is the step that made language models usable as assistants.
- It optimises a learned proxy for aggregated human preference, measured on sampled behaviour.
- It is known to introduce specific failure modes — most famously sycophancy — that look like features from the user's point of view and problems from a scientific one.
- Successors like DPO and CAI simplify the pipeline but do not address the preference-data problem at the root.

<Evidence kind="open">
If human preference data captures what users want to see rather than what is true, how do we align models to the second without destroying their usefulness on the first? The research directions worth watching are process supervision (grading reasoning steps against ground truth), verifier models, and reinforcement learning from verifiable reward signals. None has yet replaced preference data at scale.
</Evidence>
