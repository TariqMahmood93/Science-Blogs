This is a **future hypothesis**: it describes a possible scenario and states its assumptions. Nothing here reports a finding.

## The assumptions behind the question

Three assumptions shape any answer, and readers may reasonably reject each one.

1. A discovery is a claim that is new, true and useful, not just a pattern in data.
2. A claim only becomes a discovery once people can check it independently.
3. Machine learning systems will keep improving at proposing claims that are cheap to test.

<Evidence kind="interpretation">
Under these assumptions the interesting question is not whether a model can produce something novel, which is easy, but whether it can produce novel claims that survive testing more often than chance.
</Evidence>

## What counts as discovery

Philosophers have long separated the moment an idea appears from the work of showing it is right<Cite id="discovery-def" />. That distinction matters here: models are good at the first and depend on people and instruments for the second.

## Where machines already contribute

An outline for this section: survey the fields where machine learning proposes candidates (molecules, materials, proofs, protein structures) and people or experiments confirm them<Cite id="ml-science-review" />. Each example should name the system, the claim and the independent check.

## The scenario

Suppose testing becomes automated in some fields, for instance robotic labs that run experiments proposed by a model. The loop between proposing and checking would tighten, and the share of human judgement in each step would shrink<Cite id="verification-case" />.

<Evidence kind="speculation">
In that scenario, credit for a discovery might sit with the people who designed the loop and chose what to test, not with the model or the instrument alone.
</Evidence>

## What would change our mind

- Evidence that model-proposed hypotheses are confirmed no more often than those chosen by simple baselines.
- Cases where confirmation depended mostly on human reinterpretation of an output that was originally wrong.
