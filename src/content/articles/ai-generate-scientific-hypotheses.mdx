This **research idea** proposes a question and a way to test it. It is a proposal, not a result.

## Research question

Do hypotheses proposed by language models, given the same literature as a researcher, lead to tests that succeed more often than hypotheses chosen by simple baselines?

## What is currently known

Outline: summarise studies in which experts rated machine-generated hypotheses for novelty and plausibility<Cite id="hypothesis-gen-study" />. Note that ratings are not the same as experimental success.

## Proposed hypothesis

<Evidence kind="speculation">
Model-generated hypotheses will be rated as more novel than baselines but will not succeed more often when tested, because fluency rewards surprising combinations over well-grounded ones.
</Evidence>

## Rationale and supporting evidence

Novelty is easy to produce by recombination; plausibility depends on knowledge of what has already failed, which is under-reported in the literature models learn from.

## Potential method

1. Pick a field with cheap, fast experiments (for example a well-studied computational benchmark).
2. Give a model and two baselines (random recombination of keywords; hypotheses mined from paper future-work sections) the same corpus.
3. Have blinded experts rate a sample of each for novelty and feasibility<Cite id="novelty-metrics" />.
4. Run the top-rated hypotheses from each source and record which hold.

## What would support or falsify it

- **Support:** model hypotheses rated more novel, with success rates no higher than baselines.
- **Falsify:** model hypotheses succeed significantly more often than both baselines.

## Limitations and alternative explanations

- Results in one fast-feedback field may not transfer to slow, expensive sciences.
- Expert raters may recognise machine-written phrasing despite blinding.
