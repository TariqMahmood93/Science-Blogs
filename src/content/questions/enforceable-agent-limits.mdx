## Why it matters

An agent that can send email, move files or spend money can cause harm with a single wrong action. Trust depends less on how often the agent is right and more on whether its mistakes stay inside known bounds.

## What we know

<Evidence kind="established">
Instructions given to a language model influence its behaviour but do not guarantee it. Models can misread instructions, forget them over long tasks, or be redirected by text they encounter while working, a risk known as prompt injection.
</Evidence>

Software has long relied on enforcement outside the program itself: operating-system permissions, sandboxes, and approval steps for sensitive operations.

## Where the debate is

<Evidence kind="interpretation">
One position is that agents need the same kind of external enforcement: a separate layer that checks every action against a policy before it runs. The difficulty is that useful agents need broad, flexible permissions, and writing policies that are both safe and permissive enough is hard.
</Evidence>

## What would count as progress

- Policy languages that non-experts can read and check.
- Evaluations that measure how often agents violate stated constraints with and without runtime enforcement.
- Evidence that enforcement layers resist attempts to bypass them.
