## Why it matters

The answer shapes how AI tools should be released, who should have access to them, and how much organisations need to invest in defence.

## What we know

<Evidence kind="established">
Finding a vulnerability is useful to both sides: defenders can patch it, attackers can exploit it. Defenders must fix every weakness while an attacker needs only one, but defenders control their own systems and can test them continuously.
</Evidence>

## Where the debate is

<Evidence kind="interpretation">
Some argue AI helps defenders more, because it can be applied systematically across large codebases before release. Others argue it lowers the skill needed to attack, widening the pool of attackers. The balance may differ between well-resourced organisations and small ones that cannot patch quickly.
</Evidence>

## What would count as progress

- Measurements of how AI changes the time between a vulnerability's discovery and its fix, or its exploitation.
- Evidence on whether AI-found bugs differ from those found by existing tools.
- Policies for access to powerful tools that are evaluated rather than assumed to work.
